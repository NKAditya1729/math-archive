## Matrix groups (all with the subspace topology from $M_n \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$)

$M_n(\mathbb{R})$, $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, $GL_n(\mathbb{R})^+$, $SL_n(\mathbb{R})$,
$O(n)$, $SO(n)$ — L6, L11, L19. Complex analogues $M_n(\mathbb{C})$,
$GL_n(\mathbb{C})$, $SL_n(\mathbb{C})$, $U(n)$, $SU(n)$ — L6, L28.

## The three versions of $(\ast)$

He uses one symbol for all three and says so. Disambiguate in the notes.

| Version | Setting | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| $(\ast_{\mathbb{R}})$ | $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ | $\forall x \in U\ \exists \varepsilon>0$ (dep. on $x$): $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon) \subseteq U$ |
| $(\ast_{\mathbb{R}^2})$ | $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ | same with $S_\varepsilon(a,b)$ |
| $(\ast_{\mathbb{R}^n})$ | $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ | same with $S_\varepsilon(x)$ |
| $(\ast'_{\mathbb{R}^n})$ | $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ | same with $B_\varepsilon(x)$ |

## Terms, in order of definition

**topology** (L1) — $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ with (T1) $\varnothing, X
\in \tau$; (T2) closure under **finite** intersections; (T3) closure under
**arbitrary** unions.

**trivial / discrete / finite-complement topology** (L1) — $\{\varnothing,X\}$;
$\mathcal{P}(X)$; $\{U : U = \varnothing$ or $X \setminus U$ finite$\}$. The
last coincides with the discrete topology when $X$ is finite.

**open set** (L3) — $U$ is open in $\tau$ precisely when $U \in \tau$. Openness
is relative to the topology, never intrinsic. He stresses this.

**basis for a topology** (L3) — $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$ such that for every
$U \in \tau$ and $x \in U$ there is $W \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in W \subseteq
U$. **One** condition.

**the generating conditions** (L4) — for $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$
with no topology given: (i) $\bigcup_{W \in \mathcal{B}} W = X$; (ii) for
$W_1, W_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and $x \in W_1 \cap W_2$ there is $W \in \mathcal{B}$
with $x \in W \subseteq W_1 \cap W_2$. These generate $\tau_{\mathcal{B}}$, of
which $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis. **The workhorse of the whole course** — used at
L5, L6, L14, L34.

**subspace topology** (L4) — $\tau_Y = \{U \cap Y : U \in \tau\}$. The smallest
topology making the inclusion continuous (L7).

**product topology** (L5, L6) — basis $\{U \times V\}$ for two factors; for
infinite products, $\prod U_i$ with $U_i = X_i$ for all but finitely many $i$.
The smallest making all projections continuous (L7).

**box topology** (L6) — $\prod U_i$ with no finiteness condition. Strictly
finer; rejected because L9's product criterion fails for it.

**continuous** (L7) — $f^{-1}(V)$ open for every open $V$. Equivalently
(L8) for every basic open $V$; equivalently (L11) preimages of closed sets are
closed; equivalently (L15, metric spaces) $f$ preserves convergent sequences.

**homeomorphism** (L10) — bijective, continuous, with continuous inverse.

**closed set** (L11) — complement is open.

**closure** (L12) — $\overline{A} = \{x :$ every open $U \ni x$ meets $A\}$.
Closed; equals $A$ iff $A$ is closed; the smallest closed set containing $A$.

**dense** (L13) — $T$ meets every non-empty open set.

**metric space** (L14) — $d : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ with
$d(x,y)=0 \iff x=y$, symmetry, and the triangle inequality.

**connected** (L15) — not expressible as a disjoint union of two non-empty open
sets. **components** (L18) are the equivalence classes; maximal, connected,
closed.

**path connected** (L19) — any two points joined by a continuous
$\gamma : [0,1] \to X$. Stronger than connected. **path components** need not
be closed (L20).

**Hausdorff** (L23) — distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods.

**compact** (L23) — every open cover has a finite subcover. **He builds
Hausdorff into the definition** and assumes it thereafter. See known-defects.

**bounded** (L25) — contained in some ball about the origin. Heine–Borel:
in $\mathbb{R}^n$, compact $\iff$ closed and bounded.

**locally compact** (L30) — every point has an open $U$ with $\overline{U}$
compact.

**one-point compactification** (L31) — $\hat{X} = X \sqcup \{p_0\}$; open sets
are the open sets of $X$, together with complements in $\hat{X}$ of compact
subsets of $X$. Unique (L33). $\widehat{\mathbb{R}^n} = S^n$.

**quotient topology** (L34) — $U$ open in $X/\!\sim$ iff $\pi^{-1}(U)$ open.
The unique topology making $\pi$ continuous and every $\sim$-constant
continuous map descend continuously.

**topological group** (L34) — group with a topology making multiplication and
inversion continuous.

**normal** (L35) — disjoint closed sets have disjoint open neighbourhoods.
Every metric space is normal.

**regular** (L38) — a point and a disjoint closed set have disjoint open
neighbourhoods. Weaker than normal.

**second countable** (L38) — has a countable basis. Regular + second countable
$\Rightarrow$ normal.

**metrisable** (L40) — the topology comes from a metric.

## Named arguments — recurring proof patterns

Name these in the notes. Recognising them the fourth time is most of what
makes the course learnable.

**The min argument** (L2, reused L3, L10, L30). A finite intersection: get
$\varepsilon_i$ for each of finitely many sets, take $\varepsilon = \min_i
\varepsilon_i$, positive *because* the collection is finite.

**The one-witness argument** (L2, reused everywhere). An arbitrary union: the
point lies in one $U_j$, which supplies the $\varepsilon$. No minimum needed —
which is exactly why (T3) tolerates infinite index sets and (T2) does not.
Stating this contrast is one of the best supplements available early.

**The two-case split** (L1, all three examples). Separate "some set is empty"
from "none is".

**The supremum argument** (L16, reused L20, L23). Let $S$ be the set of $x$ for
which $[0,x]$ has the desired property; show $\sup S \in S$; show $\sup S = 1$
by contradiction. Used for connectedness of $[0,1]$, compactness of $[0,1]$,
and the path-component counterexample.

**The comparison lemma** (L5, reused L6, L10). $\mathcal{B}_1 \subseteq \tau_2
\Rightarrow \tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2$; both ways gives equality. The standard
tool for "these two topologies agree".

**Realise a set as a preimage** (L11, reused L25, L34). To show a set is closed
or open, exhibit it as $f^{-1}$ of something closed or open under a continuous
map. Far easier than working from the definition — he says so explicitly.

**Coordinates are polynomials, hence continuous** (L10, L11, L21, L22, L25,
L27, L34). Every matrix-group continuity argument in the course is this one
sentence.

**Induction with a group action** (L27, L28, L21). Base case small; use left
translation as a homeomorphism to move a subgroup around; conclude.
