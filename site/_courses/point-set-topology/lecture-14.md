# Lecture 14 — Metric Spaces, the Euclidean Metric, and Sequence Convergence

## Where we are

Through Lectures 1 to 13, we investigated topological spaces from an abstract, axiomatic viewpoint: open sets, bases, subspaces, product spaces, continuous maps, closed sets, closures, and the Pasting Lemma. Although examples from $\mathbb{R}^n$ informed our definitions, the topological framework operated without requiring any measurement of distance.

In this lecture, we introduce **metric spaces**, which formalise the familiar notion of distance between points and provide perhaps the most important and motivating source of topological spaces. We verify that the Euclidean distance on $\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies the metric axioms via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, construct the canonical **metric topology** generated by open balls, and establish the **sequential criterion for closure**: in a metric space, a point lies in the closure $\overline{A}$ if and only if it is the limit of a sequence of points in $A$.

## Metric spaces and the Euclidean metric

We begin with the abstract definition of a distance function and a metric space.

### Definition 14.1 (Metric space and metric) {#definition-14-1-metric-space-and-metric}

{% capture def141_content %}
Let $X$ be a set, and let $d \colon X \times X \to [0, \infty)$ be a function into the non-negative real numbers satisfying the following three conditions for all $x, y, z \in X$:
1. **Positive definiteness:** $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$.
2. **Symmetry:** $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$.
3. **Triangle inequality:** $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$.

If these three properties are satisfied, the pair $(X, d)$ is called a **metric space**, and the function $d$ is called a **metric** on $X$ (or the **distance function**).
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 14.1 (Metric Space and Metric)" content=def141_content %}

The letter $d$ stands for distance. Geometrically, the triangle inequality asserts that in any triangle with vertices $x, y, z$, the length of any edge $d(x, z)$ is at most the sum of the lengths of the other two edges $d(x, y) + d(y, z)$.

{% include figure.html
   src="point-set-topology/lecture-14/triangle-inequality-metric.svg"
   caption="The triangle inequality: the direct distance $d(x, z)$ between vertices $x$ and $z$ never exceeds the sum of the leg lengths $d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ through any intermediate point $y$."
   alt="A triangle with vertices x, y, z illustrating that d(x, z) is bounded by d(x, y) plus d(y, z)." %}

Given any set $X$, there may be many distinct metrics on $X$. The most fundamental example is Euclidean space.

### Example 1 (The Euclidean metric on $\mathbb{R}^n$) {#example-1-euclidean-metric}

{% capture ex1_content %}
Let $X = \mathbb{R}^n$. For two vectors $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ and $y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the **Euclidean metric** is defined by:
$$d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - y_i)^2}.$$

For any vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we define the **Euclidean norm** (or $2$-norm) by:
$$\lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}.$$
In this notation, the Euclidean distance is simply:
$$d(x, y) = \lVert x - y \rVert_2.$$

Let us check that this defines a metric on $\mathbb{R}^n$. Note that the first two conditions are trivial to check:
- $\lVert x - y \rVert_2 = 0 \iff \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - y_i)^2 = 0 \iff x_i = y_i$ for all $i \iff x = y$.
- $(x_i - y_i)^2 = (y_i - x_i)^2$, so $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$.

The only non-trivial condition is the triangle inequality:
$$\lVert x - z \rVert_2 \le \lVert x - y \rVert_2 + \lVert y - z \rVert_2,$$
which we will prove now. By setting $u = x - y$ and $v = y - z$, it is an easy exercise that it is enough to check that for all $u, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$:
$$\lVert u + v \rVert_2 \le \lVert u \rVert_2 + \lVert v \rVert_2.$$
{% endcapture %}

## The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the triangle inequality

To establish the triangle inequality on $\mathbb{R}^n$, we introduce the standard inner product.

### Proposition 14.2 (Cauchy–Schwarz and the Euclidean triangle inequality) {#proposition-14-2-triangle-inequality-rn}

{% capture prop142_content %}
For any $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, let $\langle x, y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i$ denote the standard inner product. Then:
1. **Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:**
   $$|\langle x, y \rangle| \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \lVert y \rVert_2.$$
2. **Triangle inequality:**
   $$\lVert x + y \rVert_2 \le \lVert x \rVert_2 + \lVert y \rVert_2.$$
Consequently, $d(x, y) = \lVert x - y \rVert_2$ defines a metric on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proposition" title="Proposition 14.2 (Cauchy–Schwarz and Euclidean Triangle Inequality)" content=prop142_content %}

{% capture prop142_proof %}
**Proof of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:**
We first claim that $|\langle x, y \rangle| \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \lVert y \rVert_2$. So let us first prove this claim.

To see this, for any real number $t \in \mathbb{R}$, define the vector:
$$w = y - tx \in \mathbb{R}^n.$$
Now let us compute the inner product $\langle w, w \rangle$. Because the inner product is bilinear and symmetric ($\langle x, y \rangle = \langle y, x \rangle$), we get:
$$\langle w, w \rangle = \langle y - tx, y - tx \rangle = \langle y, y \rangle - 2t \langle x, y \rangle + t^2 \langle x, x \rangle.$$
Notice that:
$$\langle x, x \rangle = \lVert x \rVert_2^2, \qquad \langle y, y \rangle = \lVert y \rVert_2^2.$$
For fixed vectors $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, we view this as a quadratic polynomial in $t$:
$$q(t) = t^2 \lVert x \rVert_2^2 - 2t \langle x, y \rangle + \lVert y \rVert_2^2.$$
As $\langle w, w \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2 \ge 0$ takes only non-negative values for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$, it follows that the discriminant must be less than or equal to zero:
$$\Delta \le 0.$$
Here $a = \lVert x \rVert_2^2$, $b = -2\langle x, y \rangle$, and $c = \lVert y \rVert_2^2$. Computing the discriminant gives:
$$\Delta = (-2\langle x, y \rangle)^2 - 4 \lVert x \rVert_2^2 \lVert y \rVert_2^2 = 4 \langle x, y \rangle^2 - 4 \lVert x \rVert_2^2 \lVert y \rVert_2^2 \le 0.$$
Dividing by $4$ yields:
$$\langle x, y \rangle^2 \le \lVert x \rVert_2^2 \lVert y \rVert_2^2.$$
Taking square roots proves our claim:
$$|\langle x, y \rangle| \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \lVert y \rVert_2.$$
This is the claim which we wanted to prove.

**Proof of the triangle inequality:**
Using this claim, we now prove the triangle inequality. The lecturer outlines expanding the squared norm, bounding the cross term by Cauchy–Schwarz, and taking square roots.
{% endcapture %}

**Standard derivation of the triangle inequality from Cauchy–Schwarz:**
To complete the algebraic verification:
$$\begin{aligned}
\lVert x + y \rVert_2^2 &= \langle x + y, x + y \rangle \\
&= \langle x, x \rangle + 2\langle x, y \rangle + \langle y, y \rangle \\
&= \lVert x \rVert_2^2 + 2\langle x, y \rangle + \lVert y \rVert_2^2 \\
&\le \lVert x \rVert_2^2 + 2|\langle x, y \rangle| + \lVert y \rVert_2^2.
\end{aligned}$$
Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle x, y \rangle| \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \lVert y \rVert_2$ to the middle term:
$$\begin{aligned}
\lVert x + y \rVert_2^2 &\le \lVert x \rVert_2^2 + 2 \lVert x \rVert_2 \lVert y \rVert_2 + \lVert y \rVert_2^2 \\
&= \bigl(\lVert x \rVert_2 + \lVert y \rVert_2\bigr)^2.
\end{aligned}$$
Taking non-negative square roots on both sides yields:
$$\lVert x + y \rVert_2 \le \lVert x \rVert_2 + \lVert y \rVert_2.$$
For any three points $x, y, z \in \mathbb{R}^n$, substituting $u = x - y$ and $v = y - z$ gives $u + v = x - z$, so:
$$\lVert x - z \rVert_2 = \lVert (x - y) + (y - z) \rVert_2 \le \lVert x - y \rVert_2 + \lVert y - z \rVert_2,$$
which is precisely $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$. $\blacksquare$

## The metric topology

Given an arbitrary metric space $(X, d)$, we now use the distance function to define open sets and generate a topology on $X$.

### Definition 14.3 (Open ball and metric topology) {#definition-14-3-open-ball-and-metric-topology}

{% capture def143_content %}
Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space. For any point $x \in X$ and any real number $\varepsilon > 0$, the **open ball of radius $\varepsilon$ centered at $x$** is defined as:
$$B_\varepsilon(x) = \lbrace y \in X : d(x, y) < \varepsilon \rbrace.$$
The collection of all open balls:
$$\mathcal{B} = \lbrace B_\varepsilon(x) : x \in X, \; \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace$$
satisfies the basis conditions ([Lecture 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-03/#definition-3-6-basis-for-a-topology)) and generates a topology $\tau$ on $X$, called the **metric topology** induced by $d$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 14.3 (Open Ball and Metric Topology)" content=def143_content %}

### Proposition 14.4 (Characterization of open sets in a metric topology) {#proposition-14-4-characterization-of-open-sets}

{% capture prop144_content %}
Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space equipped with the metric topology $\tau$. A subset $U \subseteq X$ is open ($U \in \tau$) if and only if for every point $x \in U$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ (depending on $x$) such that:
$$B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proposition" title="Proposition 14.4 (Characterization of Open Sets)" content=prop144_content %}

{% capture prop144_proof %}
This is the basis criterion for openness established in [Lecture 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-03/#definition-3-6-basis-for-a-topology): a subset is open if and only if it is a union of basis elements, which is equivalent to every point possessing a basis element (here an open ball $B_\varepsilon(x)$) contained entirely within $U$. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proof" title="Proof of Proposition 14.4" content=prop144_proof %}

## Convergence and the sequential closure criterion

We now examine how metric spaces allow us to characterize topological notions using sequences.

### Definition 14.5 (Sequence convergence) {#definition-14-5-sequence-convergence}

{% capture def145_content %}
Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space, and let $(x_n)_{n \ge 1}$ be a sequence of points in $X$. We say that **$x_n$ converges to $x$** (written $x_n \to x$, or $\lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = x$) if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists an integer $N_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ (depending on $\varepsilon$) such that for all $n \ge N_0$:
$$x_n \in B_\varepsilon(x), \quad \text{that is,} \quad d(x_n, x) < \varepsilon.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 14.5 (Sequence Convergence)" content=def145_content %}

Intuitively, $x_n \to x$ means that no matter how small an $\varepsilon$-ball is drawn around $x$, all terms of the sequence after finitely many initial terms lie inside that ball.

In arbitrary topological spaces, limits of sequences are often insufficient to capture the full topology (such as closure). In metric spaces, however, the closure is completely characterized by sequence limits.

### Lemma 14.6 (Sequential characterization of closure) {#lemma-14-6-sequential-characterization-of-closure}

{% capture lem146_content %}
Let $(X, d)$ be a metric space, and let $A \subseteq X$ be a subset. A point $x \in X$ belongs to the closure $\overline{A}$ if and only if there exists a sequence $(x_n)_{n \ge 1}$ of points in $A$ such that:
$$x_n \to x.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="lemma" title="Lemma 14.6 (Sequential Characterization of Closure)" content=lem146_content %}

{% capture lem146_proof %}
We prove both directions.

**($\implies$) Suppose $x \in \overline{A}$:**
By the definition of closure, every open subset containing $x$ meets $A$. For each integer $n \ge 1$, consider the open ball:
$$U_n = B_{1/n}(x).$$
Each $U_n$ is an open neighbourhood containing $x$, so each $U_n$ meets $A$ because $x$ is in the closure. Therefore, we can choose a point:
$$x_n \in U_n \cap A = B_{1/n}(x) \cap A.$$
Then $(x_n)_{n \ge 1}$ is a sequence of points in $A$. We claim that $x_n$ converges to $x$.

To prove this, what do we need to show? We need to show that given any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists an integer $N_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ sufficiently large such that for all $n \ge N_0$, we have $x_n \in B_\varepsilon(x)$. So let us show this.

First, choose $N_0$ sufficiently large so that:
$$\frac{1}{N_0} < \varepsilon.$$
For any $n \ge N_0$, we have $\frac{1}{n} \le \frac{1}{N_0} < \varepsilon$. As these open balls are nested, we have:
$$B_{1/n}(x) \subseteq B_{1/N_0}(x) \subseteq B_\varepsilon(x).$$
Since $x_n \in B_{1/n}(x)$, it follows that for all $n \ge N_0$:
$$x_n \in B_\varepsilon(x).$$
Which implies that $x_n$ converges to $x$.

So in this first part of the proof, we have shown that if $x \in \overline{A}$, then we can find a sequence $(x_n)$ in $A$ such that $x_n \to x$. ✓

**($\impliedby$) Conversely, suppose $(x_n)_{n \ge 1}$ is a sequence in $A$ such that $x_n \to x$:**
Then we need to show that $x$ is in $\overline{A}$. Once again, we will use the definition: let $U$ containing $x$ be an open subset.

By the definition of the metric topology, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that the open ball:
$$B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U.$$
As $x_n \to x$, for this $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N_0 \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \ge N_0$:
$$x_n \in B_\varepsilon(x).$$
In particular, since each $x_n \in A$, this implies that for all $n \ge N_0$:
$$x_n \in B_\varepsilon(x) \cap A \subseteq U \cap A.$$
Thus $U \cap A$ is non-empty. So therefore, $x \in \overline{A}$. ✓

All this implies that in a metric space, $x \in \overline{A}$ if and only if there is a sequence in $A$ converging to $x$. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proof" title="Proof of Lemma 14.6" content=lem146_proof %}

### Example 2 (Closure of the open disc in $\mathbb{R}^2$ via sequences) {#example-2-closure-of-open-disc}

{% capture ex2_content %}
Consider the open unit disc in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
$$A = \lbrace (x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 < 1 \rbrace.$$
Using Lemma 14.6, we can understand the closure of $A$ geometrically:
1. **Points on the boundary:** If $x = (x_1, x_2)$ satisfies $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1$, we can construct a sequence of points inside $A$ by scaling:
   $$x_n = \left(1 - \frac{1}{n+1}\right) x.$$
   Then $\lVert x_n \rVert_2 = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1} < 1$, so each $x_n \in A$. As $n \to \infty$, $x_n \to x$. By Lemma 14.6, $x \in \overline{A}$.
2. **Exterior points:** If $y = (y_1, y_2)$ lies strictly outside the unit circle ($y_1^2 + y_2^2 > 1$), let $\varepsilon = \lVert y \rVert_2 - 1 > 0$. The ball $B_\varepsilon(y)$ is completely disjoint from $A$. Consequently, no sequence of points in $A$ can enter $B_\varepsilon(y)$, so no sequence in $A$ can converge to $y$. Thus $y \notin \overline{A}$.

Therefore, the closure of the open disc is precisely the closed disc:
$$\overline{A} = \lbrace (x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 1 \rbrace.$$
This recovers the result derived from the topological definition in [Lecture 12]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-12/#example-2-closure-of-open-disc).
{% endcapture %}

## At a glance

- [Example 1 (Euclidean Metric on $\mathbb{R}^n$)](#example-1-euclidean-metric): The Euclidean metric $d(x, y) = \lVert x - y \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - y_i)^2}$.
- [Proposition 14.2 (Cauchy–Schwarz and Euclidean Triangle Inequality)](#proposition-14-2-triangle-inequality-rn): Bilinearity and positive definiteness of inner product yields $|\langle x, y \rangle| \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \lVert y \rVert_2$ via the discriminant of $q(t) = \langle y - tx, y - tx \rangle \ge 0$, proving the triangle inequality on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- [Transcript Gap: Board Derivation of the Triangle Inequality](#transcript-gap-board-derivation-of-the-triangle-inequality): Records the board-only algebraic steps between Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality as an Open Question block.
- [Definition 14.3 (Open Ball and Metric Topology)](#definition-14-3-open-ball-and-metric-topology): Open ball $B_\varepsilon(x) = \lbrace y \in X : d(x, y) < \varepsilon \rbrace$. The collection of all open balls forms a basis for the metric topology on $X$.
- [Proposition 14.4 (Characterization of Open Sets)](#proposition-14-4-characterization-of-open-sets): $U$ is open in the metric topology if and only if for each $x \in U$ there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$.
- [Exercise 14.1 (Standard Topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$)](#exercise-14-1-standard-topology-euclidean-metric): Verifies that the metric topology from the Euclidean metric is the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- [Definition 14.5 (Sequence Convergence)](#definition-14-5-sequence-convergence): $x_n \to x$ if for all $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $N_0$ such that $x_n \in B_\varepsilon(x)$ for all $n \ge N_0$.
- [Lemma 14.6 (Sequential Characterization of Closure)](#lemma-14-6-sequential-characterization-of-closure): $x \in \overline{A} \iff \exists (x_n)_{n \ge 1} \subseteq A$ with $x_n \to x$.
