# Lecture 3 — The Standard Topology on Rn, Open Sets, and Bases

## Where we are

At the close of [Lecture 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-02/#example-5-property--on-the-plane-and-disc-examples), we defined the open square $S_\varepsilon(a,b)$ and formulated property $(\ast)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, contrasting the open unit disc with the closed unit disc. The verification that the collection $\tau$ of subsets satisfying $(\ast)$ forms a genuine topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$ was left hanging.

This lecture opens by completing that proof, and then extends the construction to Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$. More fundamentally, this lecture introduces the standard vocabulary of the entire subject: naming the members of a topology **open sets**, and isolating the concept of a **basis**—a distinguished family of elementary building blocks from which every open set can be generated.

---

## Completing the standard topology on the plane

We resume where we left off at the end of Lecture 2.

Recall from [Definition 2.4 in Lecture 2]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-02/#definition-2-4-property-on-r2) that a subset $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ satisfies property $(\ast)$ if for every $(a,b) \in U$, there exists a strictly positive real number $\varepsilon > 0$ (depending on $(a,b)$) such that
$$S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq U,$$
where $S_\varepsilon(a,b) = (a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon) \times (b-\varepsilon, b+\varepsilon)$ is the open square of side length $2\varepsilon$ centered at $(a,b)$.

{% capture ex5_claim %}
Let $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ be the collection of all subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$ satisfying property $(\ast)$:
$$\tau = \lbrace U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2 \;:\; U \text{ satisfies property } (\ast) \rbrace.$$
Then $\tau$ defines a topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$, called the **standard topology** on the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="example" title="Example 5 (Standard topology on R2)" content=ex5_claim %}

We now verify that $\tau$ satisfies the three defining conditions of a topology ([Definition 1.1]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-01/#definition-1-1-topology-topological-space)).

{% capture ex5_proof %}
We check the three axioms one by one.

### Condition (T1): Empty set and whole space

We must show that $\varnothing \in \tau$ and $\mathbb{R}^2 \in \tau$.
- **The empty set $\varnothing$:** There are no points in $\varnothing$, so the condition that every point admits an open square contained in $\varnothing$ is vacuously true. Thus $\varnothing \in \tau$.
- **The whole space $\mathbb{R}^2$:** For any point $(a,b) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, we can simply choose $\varepsilon = 1 > 0$. Clearly, the open square $S_1(a,b)$ is contained in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Thus $\mathbb{R}^2$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, so $\mathbb{R}^2 \in \tau$.

Therefore, condition (T1) is satisfied.

### Condition (T2): Finite intersections

Let $U_1, U_2, \dots, U_n \in \tau$ be finitely many subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$ satisfying property $(\ast)$. We must show that their intersection
$$\bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i \in \tau.$$
That is, we must verify that the intersection satisfies property $(\ast)$.

Let $(a,b) \in \bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i$. By definition of intersection,
$$(a,b) \in U_i \quad \text{for all } i \in \lbrace 1, 2, \dots, n \rbrace.$$
Since each $U_i \in \tau$, each $U_i$ satisfies property $(\ast)$. Thus, for each $i \in \lbrace 1, \dots, n\rbrace$, there exists $\varepsilon_i > 0$ such that
$$S_{\varepsilon_i}(a,b) \subseteq U_i.$$

{% include figure.html
   src="point-set-topology/lecture-03/region-and-square-r2.svg"
   caption="A region $U_i \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ satisfying property $(\ast)$: around any point $(a,b) \in U_i$, there exists an open square $S_{\varepsilon_i}(a,b)$ lying entirely inside $U_i$."
   alt="A region U_i in the plane with an interior point (a,b) surrounded by a dashed open square that fits completely inside the region." %}

Now define
$$\varepsilon = \min \lbrace \varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \dots, \varepsilon_n \rbrace.$$
Because we are taking the minimum of **finitely many** strictly positive numbers, $\varepsilon > 0$.

Since $\varepsilon \le \varepsilon_i$ for each $i$, the open square $S_\varepsilon(a,b)$ is contained within $S_{\varepsilon_i}(a,b)$:
$$S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq S_{\varepsilon_i}(a,b) \subseteq U_i \quad \text{for all } i \in \lbrace 1, 2, \dots, n \rbrace.$$
Because $S_\varepsilon(a,b)$ is contained in each $U_i$, it is contained in their intersection:
$$S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq \bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i.$$
This proves that $\bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, so $\bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i \in \tau$. Therefore, condition (T2) is satisfied.

### Condition (T3): Arbitrary unions

Let $I$ be an arbitrary index set, and suppose that for each $i \in I$, we are given $U_i \in \tau$. We must show that
$$\bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \in \tau.$$
That is, we must show that the union satisfies property $(\ast)$.

Let $(a,b) \in \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$. By definition of union, there exists some index $j \in I$ such that
$$(a,b) \in U_j.$$
Since $U_j \in \tau$, the set $U_j$ satisfies property $(\ast)$. Therefore, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that
$$S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq U_j.$$
Since $U_j \subseteq \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$, it follows immediately that
$$S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i.$$
Thus $\bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, so $\bigcup_{i \in I} U_i \in \tau$. Therefore, condition (T3) is satisfied.

All three defining conditions hold, so $\tau$ defines a topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$, called the **standard topology** on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
{% endcapture %}

## Example 6: the standard topology on Euclidean space

Our sixth example generalises the construction from $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$ to Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ of arbitrary dimension $n \ge 1$.

Let $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be an $n$-tuple of real numbers.

{% capture def_hypercube %}
For $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, we define the **open hypercube** (or open square in $n$ dimensions) of side length $2\varepsilon$ centered at $x$, denoted $S_\varepsilon(x)$, by
$$S_\varepsilon(x) = \lbrace y = (y_1, \dots, y_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n \;:\; \lvert y_i - x_i \rvert < \varepsilon \text{ for all } i \in \lbrace 1, 2, \dots, n \rbrace \rbrace.$$
Equivalently, in terms of Cartesian products of intervals on the coordinate axes:
$$S_\varepsilon(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n (x_i - \varepsilon, \; x_i + \varepsilon) = (x_1 - \varepsilon, x_1 + \varepsilon) \times \dots \times (x_n - \varepsilon, x_n + \varepsilon).$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 3.1 (Open hypercube in Rn)" content=def_hypercube %}

{% capture def_starn %}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a subset. We say that $U$ satisfies **property $(\ast)$** (or $(\ast_{\mathbb{R}^n})$) if for every point $x \in U$, there exists a strictly positive real number $\varepsilon > 0$ (depending on $x$) such that
$$S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 3.2 (Property (*) on Rn)" content=def_starn %}

{% capture ex6_content %}
Let $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ be the collection of all subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that satisfy property $(\ast)$:
$$\tau = \lbrace U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \;:\; U \text{ satisfies property } (\ast) \rbrace.$$
Then $\tau$ defines a topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$, called the **standard topology** on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

The proof that $\tau$ defines a topology is identical in structure to the proofs for $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the lecturer leaves the formal verification as an exercise ([Exercise 3.1](#exercise-3-1-standard-topology-on-rn)).
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="example" title="Example 6 (Standard topology on Rn)" content=ex6_content %}

## Alternative formulation: open balls and property (*')

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, instead of using open hypercubes $S_\varepsilon(x)$, we can alternatively test subsets using **open balls**.

{% capture corr_ball %}
**As transcribed:** $B_\varepsilon(x) = \lbrace y \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon \rbrace$, described as "the open ball of radius $\varepsilon$".

**What is meant:** $B_\varepsilon(x) = \lbrace y \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon^2 \rbrace$, or equivalently $\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2} < \varepsilon$.

**Why:** The Euclidean distance from $y$ to $x$ is $\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2}$. The condition $\sum (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon$ defines an open ball of radius $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. For the ball to have radius $\varepsilon$, the bound on the sum of squares must be $\varepsilon^2$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="correction" title="Correction: Definition of the open ball" content=corr_ball %}

{% capture supp_ball_family %}
Notice that as $\varepsilon$ ranges over all strictly positive real numbers $(0, \infty)$, the values $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ also range over all strictly positive real numbers $(0, \infty)$. 

Consequently, the family of sets $\lbrace y : \sum (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon \rbrace_{\varepsilon > 0}$ and the family of sets $\lbrace y : \sum (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon^2 \rbrace_{\varepsilon > 0}$ are identical collections of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. The resulting topology $\tau'$ and the verification that $\tau = \tau'$ in [Exercise 3.3](#exercise-3-3-equivalence-of-tau-and-tau) are completely unaffected by this distinction.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="Equivalence of the radius parameterizations" content=supp_ball_family %}

{% capture def_ball %}
For $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, we define the **open ball of radius $\varepsilon$ centered at $x$**, denoted $B_\varepsilon(x)$, by
$$B_\varepsilon(x) = \left\lbrace y \in \mathbb{R}^n \;:\; \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon^2 \right\rbrace.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 3.3 (Open ball in Rn)" content=def_ball %}

Using open balls, we formulate an alternative property, denoted $(\ast')$ (or $(\ast'_{\mathbb{R}^n})$).

{% capture def_star_prime %}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be a subset. We say that $U$ satisfies **property $(\ast')$** if for every point $x \in U$, there exists a strictly positive real number $\varepsilon > 0$ (depending on $x$) such that
$$B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 3.4 (Property (*'))" content=def_star_prime %}

We define $\tau' \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ to be the collection of all subsets satisfying property $(\ast')$:
$$\tau' = \lbrace U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \;:\; U \text{ satisfies property } (\ast') \rbrace.$$

The lecturer poses two exercises here:
1. Show that $\tau'$ defines a topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ ([Exercise 3.2](#exercise-3-2-topology-tau-defined-by-open-balls)).
2. Show that $\tau = \tau'$ as collections of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ ([Exercise 3.3](#exercise-3-3-equivalence-of-tau-and-tau)).

{% include figure.html
   src="point-set-topology/lecture-03/square-and-disc-comparison.svg"
   caption="Mutual refinement of squares and discs in $\mathbb{R}^2$: every open square contains a concentric open ball ($B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq S_\varepsilon(x)$), and every open ball contains a concentric open square ($S_{\varepsilon/\sqrt{2}}(x) \subseteq B_\varepsilon(x)$), showing that $\tau = \tau'$."
   alt="Two diagrams: on the left, an open disc inscribed inside an open square; on the right, an open square inscribed inside an open disc." %}

{% capture supp_multiple_descriptions %}
The identity $\tau = \tau'$ delivers a major conceptual lesson: **the same topology can be described in multiple, superficially different ways**. 

Whether we test neighbourhoods using open squares $S_\varepsilon(x)$ or open balls $B_\varepsilon(x)$, the resulting collection of admissible subsets is identical. This occurs because every open hypercube contains an open ball around its center, and every open ball contains an open hypercube around its center. A topology cares only about the resulting collection of subsets, not about the geometric containers used to construct it.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="The same topology has many descriptions" content=supp_multiple_descriptions %}

## The definition of open sets

Having seen a variety of topological spaces, our overarching objective is to construct a large catalogue of spaces and investigate their mathematical properties. To proceed efficiently, we need systematic methods for defining topologies.

Before introducing these methods, the lecturer establishes the central definition of the discipline.

{% capture def_open_set %}
Let $(X, \tau)$ be a topological space. A subset $U \subseteq X$ is said to be **open in $\tau$** (or an **open subset of $X$**) if
$$U \in \tau.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 3.5 (Open set)" content=def_open_set %}

The lecturer stresses a crucial philosophical principle: **openness is strictly relative to the chosen topology $\tau$**. It is never an intrinsic property of a subset in isolation.

{% capture supp_open_relative %}
A subset $U \subseteq X$ is not "open" in the abstract; it is open *with respect to a specified topology $\tau$*. 

If we change the topology on $X$, the status of $U$ changes:
- In the discrete topology $\tau_{\text{disc}} = \mathcal{P}(X)$, *every* subset of $X$ is open.
- In the trivial topology $\tau_{\text{triv}} = \lbrace\varnothing, X\rbrace$, a proper non-empty subset $U \subsetneq X$ is *never* open.
- In $(\mathbb{R}, \tau_{\text{std}})$, the half-open interval $[0,1)$ is not open, whereas in the discrete topology on $\mathbb{R}$, $[0,1)$ is open.

Whenever we ask whether a set is open, the ambient topological space $(X, \tau)$ must be understood.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="Openness is a relative property" content=supp_open_relative %}

## The definition of a basis

Checking the three axioms (T1)–(T3) directly for an entire collection $\tau$ is often cumbersome. We now define a tool that allows a topology to be specified by a much smaller, convenient family of basic building blocks: a **basis**.

{% capture def_basis %}
Let $X$ be a set and let $\tau$ be a topology on $X$. A collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$ is called a **basis for $\tau$** if it satisfies the following condition:

For every open set $U \in \tau$ and every point $x \in U$, there exists an element $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that
$$x \in W \quad \text{and} \quad W \subseteq U.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 3.6 (Basis for a topology)" content=def_basis %}

{% include figure.html
   src="point-set-topology/lecture-03/basis-witness-w.svg"
   caption="The defining condition of a basis: for every open set $U$ and point $x \in U$, there exists a basic element $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in W \subseteq U$."
   alt="An open region U with an interior point x, and a smaller basic open set W containing x and nested inside U." %}

{% capture supp_basis_condition %}
The lecturer begins to say *"if it satisfies the following two conditions"* and immediately corrects himself: *"The following condition, that's not two."*

A basis $\mathcal{B}$ for an *already given* topology $\tau$ has exactly **one** requirement: $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$ and every point of every open set is witnessed by some $W \in \mathcal{B}$ contained in $U$. 

(The two generating conditions that allow a family $\mathcal{B}$ to *construct* a topology from scratch will be introduced in Lecture 4).
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="One condition for a basis, not two" content=supp_basis_condition %}

## First examples of bases

We now exhibit bases for the standard topologies on $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Example 1: Open intervals form a basis for R

{% capture ex_basis_r %}
Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ equipped with the standard topology $\tau$. Let $\mathcal{B}$ be the collection of all open intervals:
$$\mathcal{B} = \lbrace (a,b) \subseteq \mathbb{R} \;:\; a, b \in \mathbb{R} \cup \lbrace -\infty, +\infty \rbrace, \ a < b \rbrace.$$

**Claim.** $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\tau$.

**Proof.** First, we verify that $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$: every open interval $(a,b)$ belongs to $\tau$ (that is, every open interval is an open set). This follows by the exact same reasoning used in Lecture 2 to show that $(0,1)$ satisfies property $(\ast)$ ([Exercise 3.4](#exercise-3-4-open-intervals-belong-to-tau)). Thus $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$.

Now we check the defining condition of [Definition 3.6](#definition-3-6-basis-for-a-topology). Let $U \in \tau$ and let $x \in U$. Because $U$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that
$$(x - \varepsilon, \; x + \varepsilon) \subseteq U.$$
Define $W = (x - \varepsilon, x + \varepsilon)$. Then $W \in \mathcal{B}$ (it is an open interval), $x \in W$, and $W \subseteq U$.

Therefore, $\mathcal{B}$ satisfies the defining condition of Definition 3.6, so $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="example" title="Example 1 (Open intervals form a basis for R)" content=ex_basis_r %}

### Example 2: Open squares form a basis for R2

{% capture ex_basis_r2 %}
Let $X = \mathbb{R}^2$ equipped with the standard topology $\tau$. Let $\mathcal{B}_2$ be the collection of all open squares:
$$\mathcal{B}_2 = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2 \;:\; (a,b) \in \mathbb{R}^2, \ \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace.$$

**Claim.** $\mathcal{B}_2$ is a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Proof.** Each open square $S_\varepsilon(a,b)$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, so $\mathcal{B}_2 \subseteq \tau$. Furthermore, if $U \in \tau$ and $(a,b) \in U$, then by property $(\ast)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $S_\varepsilon(a,b) \subseteq U$. Setting $W = S_\varepsilon(a,b) \in \mathcal{B}_2$, we have $(a,b) \in W \subseteq U$. Thus $\mathcal{B}_2$ is a basis for $\tau$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="example" title="Example 2 (Open squares form a basis for R2)" content=ex_basis_r2 %}

### Example 3: Open hypercubes form a basis for Rn

{% capture ex_basis_rn %}
Let $X = \mathbb{R}^n$ equipped with the standard topology $\tau$. Let $\mathcal{B}_n$ be the collection of all open hypercubes:
$$\mathcal{B}_n = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \;:\; x \in \mathbb{R}^n, \ \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace.$$
Then $\mathcal{B}_n$ forms a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$. The verification is identical: by definition of property $(\ast)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, every point $x \in U$ is witnessed by an open hypercube $W = S_\varepsilon(x) \in \mathcal{B}_n$ contained in $U$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="example" title="Example 3 (Open hypercubes form a basis for Rn)" content=ex_basis_rn %}

## Closing and forward reference

We have defined the concept of a basis for a topology and observed how simple geometric families—open intervals, squares, and hypercubes—serve as bases for $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $\mathbb{R}^n$.

In [Lecture 4]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-04/), we will examine **two intrinsic properties** that a collection of subsets $\mathcal{B}$ must satisfy to generate a topology on its own, and prove that any collection satisfying these two properties generates a unique topology for which $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis. We will also introduce the **subspace topology**.

## Exercises

{% capture ex31_content %}
Prove that the collection $\tau \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ defined in Example 6 satisfies the three axioms (T1)–(T3), and thus defines a topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex31_sol %}
We verify the axioms:
- **(T1):** $\varnothing$ satisfies $(\ast)$ vacuously. For $\mathbb{R}^n$, given any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, choose $\varepsilon = 1$; then $S_1(x) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, so $\mathbb{R}^n \in \tau$.
- **(T2):** Let $U_1, \dots, U_k \in \tau$ and let $x \in \bigcap_{i=1}^k U_i$. For each $i \in \lbrace 1, \dots, k\rbrace$, $x \in U_i \in \tau$, so there exists $\varepsilon_i > 0$ such that $S_{\varepsilon_i}(x) \subseteq U_i$. Define $\varepsilon = \min\lbrace\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_k\rbrace > 0$. Then $S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq S_{\varepsilon_i}(x) \subseteq U_i$ for each $i$, so $S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq \bigcap_{i=1}^k U_i$. Thus the finite intersection satisfies $(\ast)$.
- **(T3):** Let $\lbrace U_i\rbrace_{i \in I} \subseteq \tau$ and let $x \in \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$. Then $x \in U_j$ for some $j \in I$. Since $U_j \in \tau$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U_j \subseteq \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$. Thus the union satisfies $(\ast)$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="exercise" title="Exercise 3.1 (Standard topology on Rn)" content=ex31_content solution=ex31_sol %}

{% capture ex32_content %}
Prove that the collection $\tau' \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ defined via property $(\ast')$ using open balls $B_\varepsilon(x)$ defines a topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex32_sol %}
- **(T1):** $\varnothing \in \tau'$ holds vacuously. For any $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, choosing $\varepsilon = 1$ gives $B_1(x) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, so $\mathbb{R}^n \in \tau'$.
- **(T2):** If $U_1, \dots, U_k \in \tau'$ and $x \in \bigcap_{i=1}^k U_i$, each $U_i$ provides $\varepsilon_i > 0$ with $B_{\varepsilon_i}(x) \subseteq U_i$. Setting $\varepsilon = \min\lbrace\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_k\rbrace > 0$, we have $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq B_{\varepsilon_i}(x) \subseteq U_i$ for all $i$, so $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq \bigcap_{i=1}^k U_i$.
- **(T3):** If $x \in \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$, then $x \in U_j$ for some $j$. Since $U_j \in \tau'$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ with $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U_j \subseteq \bigcup_{i \in I} U_i$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="exercise" title="Exercise 3.2 (Topology tau' defined by open balls)" content=ex32_content solution=ex32_sol %}

{% capture ex33_content %}
Prove that $\tau = \tau'$ as collections of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. That is, a subset $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies property $(\ast)$ if and only if it satisfies property $(\ast')$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex33_sol %}
We prove both inclusions:
1. **$\tau \subseteq \tau'$:** Suppose $U \in \tau$ and let $x \in U$. By property $(\ast)$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$. We claim that $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq S_\varepsilon(x)$. Indeed, if $y \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, then $\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2 < \varepsilon^2$. For each $i$, $(y_i - x_i)^2 \le \sum_{j=1}^n (y_j - x_j)^2 < \varepsilon^2$, which implies $\lvert y_i - x_i \rvert < \varepsilon$. Thus $y \in S_\varepsilon(x)$. Hence $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$, showing that $U$ satisfies property $(\ast')$, so $U \in \tau'$.
2. **$\tau' \subseteq \tau$:** Suppose $U \in \tau'$ and let $x \in U$. By property $(\ast')$, there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$. Define $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon / \sqrt{n} > 0$. If $y \in S_{\varepsilon'}(x)$, then $\lvert y_i - x_i \rvert < \varepsilon'$ for each $i$. Therefore:
$$\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i)^2 < \sum_{i=1}^n (\varepsilon')^2 = n (\varepsilon')^2 = n \frac{\varepsilon^2}{n} = \varepsilon^2.$$
This shows that $y \in B_\varepsilon(x)$, so $S_{\varepsilon'}(x) \subseteq B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$. Thus $U$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, so $U \in \tau$.

Combining both inclusions yields $\tau = \tau'$.
{% endcapture %}

{% capture ex34_content %}
Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R} \cup \lbrace -\infty, +\infty \rbrace$ with $a < b$. Prove that the open interval $(a,b)$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, and therefore $(a,b)$ is an open set in the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex34_sol %}
Let $x \in (a,b)$, so $a < x < b$.
- If $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ are both finite, then $x - a > 0$ and $b - x > 0$. Choose $\varepsilon = \min\lbrace (x-a)/2, (b-x)/2 \rbrace > 0$. For any $y \in (x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$, we have $y > x - \varepsilon \ge x - (x-a)/2 = (x+a)/2 > a$, and $y < x + \varepsilon \le x + (b-x)/2 = (x+b)/2 < b$. Thus $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon) \subseteq (a,b)$.
- If $a = -\infty$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}$, choose $\varepsilon = (b-x)/2 > 0$; then for $y \in (x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$, $y < b$, so $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon) \subseteq (-\infty, b)$.
- If $a \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b = +\infty$, choose $\varepsilon = (x-a)/2 > 0$; then for $y \in (x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$, $y > a$, so $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon) \subseteq (a, +\infty)$.
- If $a = -\infty$ and $b = +\infty$, then $(-\infty, +\infty) = \mathbb{R}$, which satisfies $(\ast)$ by choosing $\varepsilon = 1$.

In all cases, $(a,b)$ satisfies property $(\ast)$, so $(a,b) \in \tau$.
{% endcapture %}

## At a glance

<div class="at-a-glance-box" markdown="1">

- [Example 5 (Standard topology on R2)](#example-5-standard-topology-on-r2): Subsets satisfying property $(\ast)$ via open squares form a topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
- [Definition 3.1 (Open hypercube in Rn)](#definition-3-1-open-hypercube-in-rn): $S_\varepsilon(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n (x_i-\varepsilon, x_i+\varepsilon)$, open hypercube of side length $2\varepsilon$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- [Definition 3.2 (Property (*) on Rn)](#definition-3-2-property-on-rn): $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $(\ast)$ if $\forall x \in U\ \exists \varepsilon > 0$ such that $S_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$.
- [Example 6 (Standard topology on Rn)](#example-6-standard-topology-on-rn): $\tau = \lbrace U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n : U \text{ satisfies } (\ast)\rbrace$ is the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
- [Definition 3.3 (Open ball in Rn)](#definition-3-3-open-ball-in-rn): $B_\varepsilon(x) = \lbrace y \in \mathbb{R}^n : \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-x_i)^2 < \varepsilon^2\rbrace$, the open ball of radius $\varepsilon$.
- [Definition 3.4 (Property (*'))](#definition-3-4-property): $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $(\ast')$ if $\forall x \in U\ \exists \varepsilon > 0$ such that $B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U$; the topology $\tau'$ equals $\tau$.
- [Definition 3.5 (Open set)](#definition-3-5-open-set): A subset $U \subseteq X$ is open in $(X,\tau)$ if $U \in \tau$; openness is strictly relative to $\tau$.
- [Definition 3.6 (Basis for a topology)](#definition-3-6-basis-for-a-topology): A collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$ is a basis if $\forall U \in \tau\ \forall x \in U\ \exists W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $x \in W \subseteq U$.
- [Example 1 (Open intervals form a basis for R)](#example-1-open-intervals-form-a-basis-for-r): $\mathcal{B} = \lbrace (a,b)\rbrace$ is a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$.
- [Example 2 (Open squares form a basis for R2)](#example-2-open-squares-form-a-basis-for-r2): $\mathcal{B}_2 = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(a,b)\rbrace$ is a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
- [Example 3 (Open hypercubes form a basis for Rn)](#example-3-open-hypercubes-form-a-basis-for-rn): $\mathcal{B}_n = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(x)\rbrace$ is a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

## Further reading

**Munkres, *Topology* (2nd ed.), §13.** Introduces the formal definition of a basis for a topology, showing that open intervals generate the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ and open balls/hypercubes generate the topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Morris, *Topology Without Tears*, Chapter 2.** Sections 2.1–2.3 cover the Euclidean topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and establish the equivalence of topologies generated by open intervals, open discs, and open squares.
