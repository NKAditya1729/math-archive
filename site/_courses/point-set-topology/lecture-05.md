# Lecture 5 — The Comparison Lemma and the Product Topology

## Where we are

In [Lecture 4]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-04/), we established the two generating conditions that allow an abstract collection of subsets $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ to define a topology $\tau_{\mathcal{B}}$, and introduced the subspace topology $\tau_Y = \lbrace U \cap Y : U \in \tau \rbrace$ for any subset $Y \subseteq X$. We saw that trivial and discrete spaces restrict to trivial and discrete subspaces, and that the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ form a discrete subspace of the real line.

Here we study a geometric subspace: the real line $\mathbb{R}$ embedded into the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ as the horizontal $x$-axis. To show that this geometric embedding preserves the topology—that the subspace topology on the $x$-axis is identical to the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$—we establish two general tools: the **Comparison Lemma** (which allows one to compare two topologies by inspecting their bases) and the **subspace basis lemma**. We then use the generating conditions of Proposition 4.2 to construct the **product topology** on the Cartesian product $X \times Y$ of two topological spaces.

---

## The real line embedded in the plane

We consider the real line embedded into the plane as the horizontal coordinate axis.

Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ equipped with its standard topology $S$, and let $\mathbb{R}^2$ be equipped with its standard topology $\tau$ ([Example 5 in Lecture 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-03/#example-5-standard-topology-on-r2)). Consider the canonical inclusion map
$$i : \mathbb{R} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^2, \qquad i(x) = (x, 0).$$
Let $Y = i(\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R} \times \lbrace 0 \rbrace \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ denote the image of $i$, which is simply the horizontal $x$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

By [Definition 4.3 in Lecture 4]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-04/#definition-4-3-subspace-topology), the subset $Y$ inherits a subspace topology $\tau_Y$ from the ambient topology $\tau$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$:
$$\tau_Y = \lbrace U \cap Y \;:\; U \in \tau \rbrace.$$
Under the bijection $x \leftrightarrow (x,0)$, we may identify the subset $Y$ with $\mathbb{R}$. Through this identification, an open set $U \cap Y \in \tau_Y$ corresponds precisely to the preimage
$$i^{-1}(U) = \lbrace x \in \mathbb{R} \;:\; (x, 0) \in U \rbrace \subseteq \mathbb{R}.$$
Thus the real line carries two topologies:
1. The **standard topology** $S$ on $\mathbb{R}$ (generated by open intervals).
2. The **subspace topology** $\tau_Y$ inherited from the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.

**Claim.** The two topologies are equal: $S = \tau_Y$.

Rather than verifying this equality by a tedious hand check of arbitrary open sets, we formulate two general lemmas that will serve repeatedly throughout the course.

---

## The Comparison Lemma

When are two topologies on the same set comparable? The following lemma reduces the question of whether $\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2$ to testing the basic open sets of the smaller topology.

{% capture lem_comp %}
Let $X$ be a set, and suppose $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ are two topologies on $X$. Let $\mathcal{B}_1$ be a basis for $\tau_1$.

If every basis element of $\mathcal{B}_1$ is open in $\tau_2$ (that is, $\mathcal{B}_1 \subseteq \tau_2$), then
$$\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="lemma" title="Lemma 5.1 (The Comparison Lemma)" content=lem_comp %}

{% capture lem_comp_proof %}
So let us prove this. Our aim: let $U \subseteq X$ be an element of $\tau_1$ (that is, open in $\tau_1$). We will show that $U$ is open in $\tau_2$ ($U \in \tau_2$).

Since $\mathcal{B}_1$ is a basis for $\tau_1$, for every point $x \in U$, there exists a basic set $W_x \in \mathcal{B}_1$ such that
$$x \in W_x \subseteq U.$$
So let $I$ be the collection of such $W_x$ for every $x \in U$. Then clearly $U$ is equal to the union over the elements of $I$:
$$U = \bigcup_{x \in U} W_x.$$
This inclusion ($\bigcup_{x \in U} W_x \subseteq U$) is clear as each $W_x \subseteq U$. And the reverse inclusion ($U \subseteq \bigcup_{x \in U} W_x$) is clear because given any $x \in U$, it is in $W_x$.

Now, since each $W_x \in \mathcal{B}_1$ and $\mathcal{B}_1 \subseteq \tau_2$, this implies that $W_x \in \tau_2$—that is, each $W_x$ is open in $\tau_2$.

And since $\tau_2$ is a topology, by condition (T3) in the definition of a topology, arbitrary unions of open sets are open. This implies that
$$\bigcup_{x \in U} W_x \in \tau_2,$$
which implies that $U \in \tau_2$.

Therefore, we have proved that given any $U \in \tau_1$, $U \in \tau_2$. This implies that $\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2$, which completes the proof of the lemma. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proof" title="Proof of Lemma 5.1" content=lem_comp_proof %}

{% capture cor_comp_eq %}
Let $X$ be a set with two topologies $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$, and let $\mathcal{B}_1$ and $\mathcal{B}_2$ be bases for $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$ respectively.

If $\mathcal{B}_1 \subseteq \tau_2$ and $\mathcal{B}_2 \subseteq \tau_1$, then the two topologies are equal:
$$\tau_1 = \tau_2.$$
In particular, if $\mathcal{B}_1 = \mathcal{B}_2$, then $\tau_1 = \tau_2$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="corollary" title="Corollary 5.2 (Topological Equivalence via Bases)" content=cor_comp_eq %}

{% capture supp_finer_coarser %}
When $\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2$, we say that $\tau_2$ is **finer** (or **stronger**) than $\tau_1$, and that $\tau_1$ is **coarser** (or **weaker**) than $\tau_2$.

The Comparison Lemma states that to prove a topology $\tau_2$ contains all open sets of $\tau_1$, it is necessary and sufficient that $\tau_2$ contain merely the *basic* open sets of $\tau_1$. This reduces an assertion about all possible open sets to an assertion about a simpler, manageable generating family.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="Finer and coarser topologies" content=supp_finer_coarser %}

## A basis for the subspace topology

Our second tool shows how a basis for an ambient space automatically yields a basis for any of its subspaces.

{% capture lem_subspace_basis %}
Let $(X,\tau)$ be a topological space, let $Y \subseteq X$ be a subset with subspace topology $\tau_Y$, and let $\mathcal{B}$ be a basis for $\tau$.

Then the collection of intersections
$$\mathcal{B}_Y = \lbrace W \cap Y \;:\; W \in \mathcal{B} \rbrace$$
is a basis for the subspace topology $\tau_Y$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="lemma" title="Lemma 5.3 (Basis for a Subspace)" content=lem_subspace_basis %}

{% capture lem_subspace_basis_proof %}
We check the requirements of [Definition 3.6 in Lecture 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-03/#definition-3-6-basis-for-a-topology):

1. **$\mathcal{B}_Y \subseteq \tau_Y$:** Let $W \in \mathcal{B}$. Since $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$, $W$ is open in $X$. By Definition 4.3, $W \cap Y \in \tau_Y$. Thus $\mathcal{B}_Y \subseteq \tau_Y$.
2. **The basis condition:** Let $V \in \tau_Y$ and let $y \in V$. By Definition 4.3, $V = U \cap Y$ for some open set $U \in \tau$. Since $y \in V = U \cap Y$, we have $y \in U$. Because $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\tau$, there exists an element $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that
   $$y \in W \subseteq U.$$
   Intersecting this inclusion with $Y$, we obtain
   $$y \in W \cap Y \subseteq U \cap Y = V.$$
   Setting $W' = W \cap Y$, we have $W' \in \mathcal{B}_Y$ with $y \in W' \subseteq V$.

Therefore, $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is a basis for $\tau_Y$. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proof" title="Proof of Lemma 5.3" content=lem_subspace_basis_proof %}

## Equivalence of the standard and subspace topologies on R

We can now prove our claim that the real line as the horizontal axis inherits the standard topology of $\mathbb{R}$.

{% capture prop_axis_subspace %}
Let $Y = \mathbb{R} \times \lbrace 0 \rbrace \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ be the $x$-axis equipped with the subspace topology $\tau_Y$ from the standard topology $\tau$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Let $S$ be the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$.

Under the identification $x \leftrightarrow (x,0)$, the subspace topology $\tau_Y$ is equal to the standard topology $S$:
$$\tau_Y = S.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proposition" title="Proposition 5.4 (Subspace Topology on the Real Line in R2)" content=prop_axis_subspace %}

{% include figure.html
   src="point-set-topology/lecture-05/square-intersects-axis.svg"
   caption="Open squares in $\mathbb{R}^2$ intersecting the horizontal axis $Y$: a square disjoint from the axis gives $\varnothing$, while a square meeting the axis traces out the open interval $(a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon)$ on the line."
   alt="The plane showing the horizontal axis with one square missing the axis and a second square centered on the axis intersecting it in an open interval." %}

{% capture prop_axis_proof %}
By [Example 2 in Lecture 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-03/#example-2-open-squares-form-a-basis-for-r2), the standard topology $\tau$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ has as a basis the collection of open squares:
$$\mathcal{B} = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(a,b) \;:\; (a,b) \in \mathbb{R}^2, \ \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace,$$
where $S_\varepsilon(a,b) = (a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon) \times (b-\varepsilon, b+\varepsilon)$.

By [Lemma 5.3](#lemma-5-3-basis-for-a-subspace), a basis for the subspace topology $\tau_Y$ is given by
$$\mathcal{B}_Y = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(a,b) \cap Y \;:\; (a,b) \in \mathbb{R}^2, \ \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace.$$

On the other hand, let $\mathcal{C}$ be the collection of intervals $B_\varepsilon(x) = (x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon)$:
$$\mathcal{C} = \lbrace (x-\varepsilon, \; x+\varepsilon) \;:\; x \in \mathbb{R}, \ \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace \cup \lbrace \varnothing \rbrace.$$
Then $\mathcal{C}$ is a basis for the standard topology $S$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

We want to show that $S = \tau_Y$. By [Corollary 5.2](#corollary-5-2-topological-equivalence-via-bases), it is enough to show that $\mathcal{B}_Y = \mathcal{C}$. Let us check that $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is indeed equal to $\mathcal{C}$.

Let us examine what the elements of $\mathcal{B}_Y$ look like:
- The horizontal axis is $Y = \lbrace (x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y = 0 \rbrace$.
- For every square $S_\varepsilon(a,b)$, either it does not intersect the axis ($S_\varepsilon(a,b) \cap Y = \varnothing$, when $\lvert b \rvert \ge \varepsilon$), or it intersects the axis in $(a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon)$ (when $\lvert b \rvert < \varepsilon$).

Identifying the $x$-axis with $\mathbb{R}$ via the embedding $i$, each non-empty intersection is identified with the interval $(a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon) \in \mathcal{C}$. Thus it is clear that $\mathcal{B}_Y \subseteq \mathcal{C}$.

Conversely, if we take any element $(x-\varepsilon, x+\varepsilon) \in \mathcal{C}$, we can take the open square centered directly on the axis at $(x,0)$:
$$S_\varepsilon(x, 0) \cap Y = (x-\varepsilon, \; x+\varepsilon).$$
And the empty set is also obtained by taking a square disjoint from the axis (for instance $S_1(0,2) \cap Y = \varnothing$). Thus $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{B}_Y$.

Therefore, we have proved both inclusions, which implies that both these bases are equal ($\mathcal{B}_Y = \mathcal{C}$), which implies that the subspace topology is equal to the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$:
$$\tau_Y = S. \quad \blacksquare$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proof" title="Proof of Proposition 5.4" content=prop_axis_proof %}

{% capture supp_hyperplane %}
The exact same reasoning generalises to higher dimensions.

For instance, we can embed $\mathbb{R}^2$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$ ($n \ge 3$) as the coordinate hyperplane:
$$i : \mathbb{R}^2 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad (x_1, x_2) \mapsto (x_1, x_2, 0, \dots, 0).$$
An open hypercube $S_\varepsilon(a_1, \dots, a_n) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ either misses the hyperplane or intersects it in an open square $S_\varepsilon(a_1, a_2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. Applying Lemma 5.3 and Corollary 5.2 establishes that the subspace topology on the coordinate hyperplane is identical to the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$ ([Exercise 5.4](#exercise-5-4-hyperplane-subspaces-in-rn)).
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="Generalization: Hyperplane subspaces in Euclidean space" content=supp_hyperplane %}

## The product topology

We now turn to the second major construction of the lecture: given two topological spaces, how do we equip their Cartesian product with a natural topology?

Let $(X,\tau_X)$ and $(Y,\tau_Y)$ be two topological spaces. The Cartesian product is the set of ordered pairs:
$$X \times Y = \lbrace (x,y) \;:\; x \in X, \ y \in Y \rbrace.$$

We wish to define a topology on $X \times Y$. An open set in the product will not generally be a single Cartesian rectangle $U \times V$, just as an open disc in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not a single product of open intervals. Instead, we use products of open sets to form a **basis**, and use [Proposition 4.2 in Lecture 4]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-04/#proposition-4-2-the-generating-proposition) to generate the topology.

{% capture lem_prod_basis %}
Let $(X,\tau_X)$ and $(Y,\tau_Y)$ be topological spaces. Define the collection
$$\mathcal{B} = \lbrace U \times V \;:\; U \in \tau_X, \ V \in \tau_Y \rbrace \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X \times Y).$$
Then $\mathcal{B}$ satisfies the two conditions in the proposition on generating topologies:
1. $\bigcup_{W \in \mathcal{B}} W = X \times Y$.
2. For any $W_1, W_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and any point $(a,b) \in W_1 \cap W_2$, there exists $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that
   $$(a,b) \in W \subseteq W_1 \cap W_2.$$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="lemma" title="Lemma 5.5 (Generating Conditions for Products)" content=lem_prod_basis %}

{% include figure.html
   src="point-set-topology/lecture-05/product-basis-rectangle.svg"
   caption="Intersection of two basic open rectangles: $(U_1 \times V_1) \cap (U_2 \times V_2) = (U_1 \cap U_2) \times (V_1 \cap V_2)$, which is itself a basic open rectangle in $\mathcal{B}$."
   alt="Two intersecting rectangles in X x Y with coordinate projections showing the intersection is formed by the intersection of horizontal projections and vertical projections." %}

{% capture lem_prod_proof %}
Recall the two conditions we need to check:
- **(A):** When we take the union of all $W \in \mathcal{B}$, we get the entire set $X \times Y$.
- **(B):** Suppose $W_1, W_2 \in \mathcal{B}$, and $(a,b)$ is an element in the intersection $W_1 \cap W_2$; then there is a $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $(a,b) \in W$ and $W \subseteq W_1 \cap W_2$.

Let us check that these two conditions are satisfied.

**Condition (A):**
Since $X \in \tau_X$ and $Y \in \tau_Y$, this implies from the definition of $\mathcal{B}$ that $X \times Y \in \mathcal{B}$. Therefore, clearly, the union $\bigcup_{W \in \mathcal{B}} W$ contains $X \times Y$, and of course it is a subset of $X \times Y$.

Therefore, this union is actually equal to $X \times Y$. So this proves (A); this proves that (A) is indeed true. ✓

**Condition (B):**
We are given $W_1, W_2 \in \mathcal{B}$. This implies from the definition of $\mathcal{B}$ that
$$W_1 = U_1 \times V_1 \quad \text{and} \quad W_2 = U_2 \times V_2,$$
where $U_1, U_2 \in \tau_X$ and $V_1, V_2 \in \tau_Y$.

Let $(a,b) \in W_1 \cap W_2 = (U_1 \times V_1) \cap (U_2 \times V_2)$. This implies that $a \in U_1 \cap U_2$ and $b \in V_1 \cap V_2$.

Now, since $\tau_X$ and $\tau_Y$ are topologies, note that $U_1 \cap U_2 \in \tau_X$ and $V_1 \cap V_2 \in \tau_Y$ by condition (T2). Thus we have:
$$(a,b) \in (U_1 \cap U_2) \times (V_1 \cap V_2),$$
which is an element of $\mathcal{B}$! And this set is contained in $U_i \times V_i$ for $i = 1, 2$, so
$$(a,b) \in (U_1 \cap U_2) \times (V_1 \cap V_2) \subseteq W_1 \cap W_2.$$
Therefore, we can take $W = (U_1 \cap U_2) \times (V_1 \cap V_2) \in \mathcal{B}$.

So given any $(a,b)$ in the intersection, we have found a $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $(a,b) \in W$ and $W \subseteq W_1 \cap W_2$.

So this proves that $\mathcal{B}$ satisfies the two conditions to generate a topology. And so it generates a topology, which we denote $\tau_{\mathcal{B}}$, which has $\mathcal{B}$ as a basis. This completes the proof of the lemma. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="proof" title="Proof of Lemma 5.5" content=lem_prod_proof %}

With the generating conditions verified, we formally define the product topology.

{% capture def_prod_topo %}
Let $(X,\tau_X)$ and $(Y,\tau_Y)$ be topological spaces. The **product topology** on the Cartesian product $X \times Y$ is the topology generated by the basis
$$\mathcal{B} = \lbrace U \times V \;:\; U \in \tau_X, \ V \in \tau_Y \rbrace.$$
A subset $W \subseteq X \times Y$ is open in the product topology if and only if for every $(x,y) \in W$, there exist open sets $U \in \tau_X$ and $V \in \tau_Y$ such that
$$(x,y) \in U \times V \subseteq W.$$
Equivalently, every open set in the product topology is an arbitrary union of open rectangles $U \times V$.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="definition" title="Definition 5.6 (Product topology on X x Y)" content=def_prod_topo %}

{% capture supp_prod_rectangles %}
It is essential to understand that not every open set in $X \times Y$ is of the product form $U \times V$.

For example, in $\mathbb{R}^2 = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$, an open circular disc $D = \lbrace (x,y) : x^2 + y^2 < 1 \rbrace$ cannot be written as $U \times V$ for any subsets $U, V \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ (the product of two subsets is always a rectangle with sides parallel to the axes). However, $D$ *is* an open set in the product topology, because around every point $(x,y) \in D$ there exists an open rectangle (for instance, an open square) contained in $D$. The open disc is the union of all such open rectangles.
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="supplement" title="Basic rectangles versus general open sets" content=supp_prod_rectangles %}

## Closing and forward reference

We have established the Comparison Lemma, proven that the subspace topology on the coordinate axis $\mathbb{R} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ equals the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$, and defined the product topology on the product of two spaces $X \times Y$.

In [Lecture 6]({{ site.baseurl }}/point-set-topology/lecture-06/), we will:
1. Extend products to arbitrary (possibly infinite) families of topological spaces $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$.
2. Contrast the **box topology** with the **product topology** on infinite products, seeing why the product topology requires all but finitely many coordinate factors to be the entire space $X_i$.
3. Prove that the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^n = \mathbb{R} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{R}$ coincides with the standard Euclidean topology.
4. Survey a rich catalogue of topological spaces, including spheres $S^1, S^n$, matrix groups $GL_n(\mathbb{R}), SL_n(\mathbb{R}), O(n), SO(n)$, and their complex analogues.

## Exercises

{% capture ex51_content %}
Let $(X,\tau)$ be a topological space and let $Y \subseteq X$. Let $\mathcal{B}$ be a basis for $\tau$. Prove directly that the collection
$$\mathcal{B}_Y = \lbrace W \cap Y \;:\; W \in \mathcal{B} \rbrace$$
satisfies the two generating conditions of Proposition 4.2 on $Y$, and that the topology it generates on $Y$ is precisely the subspace topology $\tau_Y$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex51_sol %}
We verify the two generating conditions for $\mathcal{B}_Y$ on the set $Y$:

1. **Covering condition:** By Lemma 4.1, $\bigcup_{W \in \mathcal{B}} W = X$. Intersecting both sides with $Y$:
   $$\bigcup_{W' \in \mathcal{B}_Y} W' = \bigcup_{W \in \mathcal{B}} (W \cap Y) = \left(\bigcup_{W \in \mathcal{B}} W\right) \cap Y = X \cap Y = Y.$$
2. **Intersection refinement:** Let $W'_1, W'_2 \in \mathcal{B}_Y$ and $y \in W'_1 \cap W'_2$. By definition, $W'_1 = W_1 \cap Y$ and $W'_2 = W_2 \cap Y$ for some $W_1, W_2 \in \mathcal{B}$. Then
   $$y \in (W_1 \cap Y) \cap (W_2 \cap Y) = (W_1 \cap W_2) \cap Y.$$
   In particular, $y \in W_1 \cap W_2$. Since $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $(X,\tau)$, there exists $W \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $y \in W \subseteq W_1 \cap W_2$. Setting $W' = W \cap Y \in \mathcal{B}_Y$, we have
   $$y \in W' = W \cap Y \subseteq (W_1 \cap W_2) \cap Y = W'_1 \cap W'_2.$$

Thus $\mathcal{B}_Y$ satisfies both generating conditions, so it generates a topology $\tau_{\mathcal{B}_Y}$ on $Y$. By Lemma 5.3, $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is a basis for $\tau_Y$. By Exercise 4.2, the topology generated by a basis of a space is the space's topology itself. Hence $\tau_{\mathcal{B}_Y} = \tau_Y$. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
{% include block.html type="exercise" title="Exercise 5.1 (Direct verification of the subspace basis)" content=ex51_content solution=ex51_sol %}

{% capture ex52_content %}
Let $(X,\tau_X)$ and $(Y,\tau_Y)$ be topological spaces. Suppose $\mathcal{B}_X$ is a basis for $\tau_X$ and $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is a basis for $\tau_Y$. Prove that the smaller collection
$$\mathcal{B}' = \lbrace B_1 \times B_2 \;:\; B_1 \in \mathcal{B}_X, \ B_2 \in \mathcal{B}_Y \rbrace$$
is a basis for the product topology on $X \times Y$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex52_sol %}
Let $\tau_{\text{prod}}$ be the product topology on $X \times Y$. We apply the Comparison Lemma (or verify the basis condition directly).

1. **$\mathcal{B}' \subseteq \tau_{\text{prod}}$:** For each $B_1 \in \mathcal{B}_X \subseteq \tau_X$ and $B_2 \in \mathcal{B}_Y \subseteq \tau_Y$, the product $B_1 \times B_2$ belongs to the defining basis $\mathcal{B} = \lbrace U \times V : U \in \tau_X, V \in \tau_Y \rbrace \subseteq \tau_{\text{prod}}$. Thus $\mathcal{B}' \subseteq \tau_{\text{prod}}$.
2. **The basis condition:** Let $W \in \tau_{\text{prod}}$ and let $(x,y) \in W$. By Definition 5.6, there exist $U \in \tau_X$ and $V \in \tau_Y$ such that
   $$(x,y) \in U \times V \subseteq W.$$
   This means $x \in U$ and $y \in V$. Since $\mathcal{B}_X$ is a basis for $\tau_X$, there exists $B_1 \in \mathcal{B}_X$ with $x \in B_1 \subseteq U$. Since $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is a basis for $\tau_Y$, there exists $B_2 \in \mathcal{B}_Y$ with $y \in B_2 \subseteq V$.
   Then
   $$(x,y) \in B_1 \times B_2 \subseteq U \times V \subseteq W.$$
   Since $B_1 \times B_2 \in \mathcal{B}'$, this proves that $\mathcal{B}'$ satisfies the basis condition for $\tau_{\text{prod}}$.

Therefore, $\mathcal{B}'$ is a basis for the product topology on $X \times Y$. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}

{% capture ex53_content %}
Let $X, Y, Z$ be topological spaces. Prove that the natural bijection
$$f : (X \times Y) \times Z \to X \times (Y \times Z), \qquad ((x,y), z) \mapsto (x, (y,z))$$
is a topological isomorphism (that is, $U$ is open in $(X \times Y) \times Z$ if and only if $f(U)$ is open in $X \times (Y \times Z)$).
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex53_sol %}
By Exercise 5.2, a basis for $(X \times Y) \times Z$ is given by sets of the form
$$(U \times V) \times W,$$
where $U \in \tau_X$, $V \in \tau_Y$, and $W \in \tau_Z$.
Under the map $f$, the image of such a basic set is
$$f\bigl((U \times V) \times W\bigr) = U \times (V \times W).$$
By Exercise 5.2 applied to $X$ and $Y \times Z$, sets of the form $U \times (V \times W)$ form a basis for $X \times (Y \times Z)$.
Since $f$ establishes a bijection between the basis elements of the two product spaces, and arbitrary unions are preserved by bijections, $f$ maps open sets to open sets and preimages of open sets are open. Thus the product operation is strictly associative. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}

{% capture ex54_content %}
Let $n \ge 3$, and let $\mathbb{R}^2$ be embedded into $\mathbb{R}^n$ as the coordinate hyperplane
$$Y = \lbrace (x_1, x_2, 0, \dots, 0) \in \mathbb{R}^n \;:\; x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R} \rbrace.$$
Prove that the subspace topology $\tau_Y$ inherited from the standard Euclidean topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ coincides with the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
{% endcapture %}
{% capture ex54_sol %}
By Definition 3.1 and Example 6 in Lecture 3, a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ consists of open hypercubes:
$$S_\varepsilon(a) = \prod_{i=1}^n (a_i - \varepsilon, \; a_i + \varepsilon), \qquad a = (a_1, \dots, a_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n, \ \varepsilon > 0.$$
By Lemma 5.3, a basis for $\tau_Y$ is $\mathcal{B}_Y = \lbrace S_\varepsilon(a) \cap Y : a \in \mathbb{R}^n, \varepsilon > 0 \rbrace$.
A point $(x_1, x_2, 0, \dots, 0)$ belongs to $S_\varepsilon(a)$ if and only if:
- $\lvert x_1 - a_1 \rvert < \varepsilon$ and $\lvert x_2 - a_2 \rvert < \varepsilon$, and
- $\lvert 0 - a_i \rvert < \varepsilon$ for all $i = 3, 4, \dots, n$.

If $\max_{i=3,\dots,n} \lvert a_i \rvert \ge \varepsilon$, then $S_\varepsilon(a) \cap Y = \varnothing$.
If $\max_{i=3,\dots,n} \lvert a_i \rvert < \varepsilon$, then
$$S_\varepsilon(a) \cap Y = (a_1 - \varepsilon, a_1 + \varepsilon) \times (a_2 - \varepsilon, a_2 + \varepsilon) \times \lbrace 0 \rbrace^{n-2} \cong S_\varepsilon(a_1, a_2) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2.$$
Every non-empty element of $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is an open square in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and every open square $S_\varepsilon(a_1, a_2)$ is obtained by taking $a = (a_1, a_2, 0, \dots, 0)$.
Thus $\mathcal{B}_Y$ is identical to the standard basis of open squares for $\mathbb{R}^2$ (plus $\varnothing$).
By Corollary 5.2, $\tau_Y$ is equal to the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$. $\blacksquare$
{% endcapture %}
